## Day 25 · Ideas That Don't Die in Chat

### 0:00 · The someday pile
Innovation is the thing you'll get to when things calm down. Things never calm down.
Someone on your team spots a better way to do something. They drop it in chat. Two people react. Then the thread moves on, and the idea is gone.
Ask them six months later and they'll shrug. Not because the idea was bad. Because it had nowhere to live. That's how a company teaches its people to stop suggesting things.

### 0:40 · Small ideas, tested fast
Two frameworks fixed this for us. Kaizen, the Toyota idea: improvement comes in small steps, from the people closest to the work, because they see the waste first.
And Lean Startup, from Eric Ries: don't debate an idea, test it. Build the smallest version, measure what happens, learn.
Both need the same thing to work. A place where ideas get captured, seen, and picked up. Not a suggestion box nobody opens. A feed the whole company reads.

### 1:20 · The ideas feed in 8 Edges
Here's ours. This is the ideas feed in 8 Edges, and anyone in the company can post to it.
Two kinds of posts. Build ideas: something we should make or change, and the AI helps shape it into a real plan. And learnings: something someone figured out this week that the rest of us should know.
On the admin side, leadership sees every idea in one place and picks what to test next. The AI does the capturing and the shaping. A human still decides what gets built. Nothing scrolls away.

### 2:30 · Close
Every idea gets a home. The best ones get tested, small and fast. And your people learn that suggesting things is worth it. That's an innovative culture.
Tomorrow: everyone's busy, nothing's moving, and how goals fix that. See you then.

## Day 26 · Goal Setting

### 0:00 · Busy is not moving
Walk your floor. Every calendar is full. Every person is working hard. And the company is not moving.
Now ask five people what the top goal is this quarter. You'll get five different answers. That's the tell. Activity without a shared target isn't progress. It's motion.
Here's a confession about our own company: we did goals last. On purpose. Because a goal system without data underneath it is theater.

### 0:40 · Pick, then measure
Three pieces of research shaped how we set goals. Richard Rumelt: a long list of goals is not a strategy. Good strategy picks a few things and says no to the rest.
John Doerr, the OKR playbook: every goal gets a number, so you know if you hit it.
And Locke and Latham, decades of goal-setting research: specific, hard goals beat "do your best" every single time. Few goals. Real numbers. Ambitious targets. That's the recipe.

### 1:20 · Setting goals in 8 Edges
Here's how that lives in 8 Edges. The strategy sits at the top of the page, one banner. Company goals under it. Each office's goals under those.
Every goal carries a number, and exactly one accountable human name. Even the goals where an agent does most of the work. Agents execute. Accountability never delegates to software.
And the system refuses to save a goal that doesn't connect to the level above it. Nothing floats free. If a goal can't explain which outcome it serves, it doesn't get set.

### 2:30 · Close
Few goals, real numbers, one name on each. Revenue, talent, operations, innovation: all four outcomes pointed at the same strategy, instead of four teams pulling in four directions.
Tomorrow: the meeting that keeps them pointed there, every single Monday. See you then.

## Day 27 · The Monday Sync

### 0:00 · The meeting that reports
Think about your Monday meeting. An hour long. Forty-five minutes of it is people reading numbers to each other.
By the time you reach the one thing that actually needs a decision, the hour is up. So the decision waits a week. Then another.
That's a meeting that reports instead of decides. And it's not a discipline problem. It's that gathering the status costs so much, there's nothing left for judgment.

### 0:40 · Frequently discussed
Back on Day 2 I showed you FAST goals, from Sull and Sull at MIT Sloan. Frequently discussed, ambitious, specific, transparent.
The word that matters most is frequently. A goal reviewed quarterly is filed. A goal reviewed weekly steers.
But frequency has a price: someone has to pull the numbers every week. That used to be the blocker. Now it's the part you never see happen.

### 1:20 · The Monday packet in 8 Edges
Here's our Monday. The packet is already on the table when we walk in. Agents pulled the week's numbers from the source systems overnight.
What moved. What stalled. And when a metric misses two weeks running, the system files an issue on its own and puts it on the agenda.
So the humans walk in at the decision point, not the data-gathering point. Thirty minutes. Almost all of it judgment. The AI reads the status. Your people decide what to do about it.

### 2:30 · Close
Every Monday, all four outcomes get checked against the same strategy. That's how they stay pointed at it: not a yearly offsite, a weekly habit that costs thirty minutes.
Tomorrow: what happens when a number misses, and why blame is the most expensive response. See you then.

## Day 28 · Reviews: Misses Are Data

### 0:00 · The quiet room
A number misses. The room goes quiet. And everyone starts rehearsing their defense instead of thinking about the problem.
That's what blame does. It doesn't make people careful. It makes them careful about what they tell you. In a blame culture, the truth arrives last, if it arrives at all.
And now there's a twist your review process was never built for. Half the misses aren't human anymore.

### 0:40 · Safe enough to learn
Amy Edmondson at Harvard studied this for decades. Teams that feel safe reporting mistakes learn faster, because the mistakes surface while they're still cheap. A miss isn't a verdict. A miss is data.
So every review asks three questions, in order. Was the goal wrong? Was the system constrained? Was the execution weak? Only the third one is about the person, and even then the conversation runs on GROW, so it points forward, not backward.

### 1:20 · Reviews in 8 Edges
Here's the loop in 8 Edges. The miss is already filed as an issue, with the numbers attached. Nobody has to confess. The data walked in first.
If it's a person, the manager's next 1-on-1 brief arrives pre-structured with GROW, miss included, so the conversation is coaching, not ambush.
And yes, the agents get reviewed too. Same three questions. Sometimes the fix is coaching a person. Sometimes it's rewriting an agent's instructions. Sometimes it's moving the work from one to the other.

### 2:30 · Close
When misses are data, reviews stop being trials and start being tuning. People, agents, goals: all four outcomes get corrected back toward the same strategy, every cycle.
Tomorrow: the receipts. Our real numbers after thirty days, on screen. See you then.

## Day 30 · Two Skills, Three Doors

### 0:00 · The honest question
Thirty days ago you watched a company nobody could see. Since then you've watched it get rebuilt, workflow by workflow, on real screens.
Which leaves one honest question: could you do this yourself?
The answer starts with a distinction most people miss. And getting it wrong wastes years.

### 0:45 · Two different skills
Running a company on AI takes two very different skills, and almost everyone confuses them.
The first is app development. The database, the screens, the agents, the security, the thousand small decisions that make software real. It's a deep profession. It's also, for you, a distraction: every month your team spends learning it is a month not spent on your actual business.
The second is process excellence. Knowing which workflows matter in your company, who should own each step, what good looks like, and what to measure. Nobody can outsource that one, because nobody knows your business but you.
Here's our answer to that split: we handled most of the app development for you. 8 Edges ships with the database, the four offices, the agents, and the workflows you've watched all season, already built, already running our own company. What's left is the part only you can do.

### 1:30 · Three doors
So there are three doors from here.
Door one: free. Do it yourself. Everything in this season, the frameworks, the order, the patterns, is open to study. If you have the builder gene in-house, run.
Door two: assisted, 99 dollars a month. The AI Officer Institute: the curriculum, the community, and our team beside you while you build your own version, week by week. You bring the process excellence. We teach the rest.
Door three: we build it with you, 15,000 dollars. Our engineers design ten custom workflows around your business, connect five data sources into one home, and hand it to you live and running.
Whichever door you pick, the order never changes. Data first. Workflows second. Rhythm last.
