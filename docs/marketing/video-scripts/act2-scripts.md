## Day 08 · One Database

### 0:00 · Where does the deal stand?
Ask a simple question about a deal your team has worked for a month. Where does it stand?
The answer takes three people, two spreadsheets, and an email thread you're not on. Nobody's lying. Nobody's lazy. The information exists. It just lives in so many places that nobody can see the company you're running.
It's not a people problem. It's a places problem.
So here's the rule that changed our company more than any technology. If it's not in the system, it didn't happen.

### 0:40 · A single source of truth
The framework is old and boring: a single source of truth. One database for the whole company. Not a warehouse project. Not forty tools duct-taped together. One home, where a customer is one record, a person is one record, a deal is one record, and everything that happens to them attaches to that record.
What does the rule mean in practice? The deal that lives in an inbox does not exist. The feedback delivered in a hallway does not exist. Harsh? Sure. It's also the moment your company becomes visible.
Stanford studied fifty one successful AI deployments and found only 6 percent of companies had data that was ready for AI at all. This rule is the entry fee to that club. And it's a leadership commitment, not a software feature.

### 1:20 · One record, everything attached
This is our real company inside 8 Edges. One database, more than 150 tables. 900 people, 255 companies, 136 deals, all living in one place.
Watch what happens when I open one client. Their deals are right there. Every meeting we've had. Every invoice we've sent. Nothing in an inbox. Nothing in someone's head.
The AI reads all of it. Your people decide what it means. When every fact has one home, AI stops being a toy, because it can finally see the company. Ask it a question and it answers in seconds, because the answer only lives in one place. We'll do exactly that on day ten. Everything we build for the next three weeks stands on this.

### 2:30 · Close
One home for every fact. One rule: if it's not in the system, it didn't happen. Boring? Completely. That's the point. That's the foundation.

## Day 09 · Anatomy of a Designed Workflow

### 0:00 · Nobody wrote down who does what
Ask anyone in your company how a sales call becomes a proposal. Or how a new hire gets their laptop. You'll get a shrug, or a story.
The process lives in people's heads. Nobody wrote down who does what. So every workflow runs on memory and goodwill, and the busiest week decides what slips.
And when something does slip, you can't even ask why. There was no design to compare it to. Just a vague sense that someone should have done something.

### 0:40 · The playbook, written twice
The framework is the playbook, and it gets written twice. Human-readable, so your people know exactly which steps are theirs. And agent-executable, so the AI knows exactly which steps are its.
Every step carries a name. A person, or an agent. Never "the team". When a human owns a step, the playbook says what they decide. When the AI owns a step, the playbook says what it produces. Humans decide, the AI produces, and the design says which is which. That split is the whole anatomy.
Most companies skip this because it feels like paperwork. It's the opposite. One page per workflow is enough. And that page is what lets you hand half the work to software without losing control of any of it.

### 1:20 · One real playbook in 8 Edges
Here's one real playbook in 8 Edges: a sales call becoming a proposal.
The human owns the call, the relationship, and the price. Then one administrative act: the transcript goes into the system. Everything after that line is the AI's job. It reads the call, updates the client record, moves the deal on this board, and drafts a proposal page on our own domain.
Notice what the human never touches anymore. No write-up. No formatting. No chasing. And notice what the AI never touches. The relationship. The price. The judgment.
Every step you just saw has exactly one name on it. On day twelve you'll watch this run end to end, against the clock. Today, just notice the anatomy: the AI reads and prepares, your people judge and decide, and none of it depends on anyone's memory.

### 2:30 · Close
Write down who does what. Put a name on every step. Start with the worst workflow you have. That's the foundation.

## Day 20 · The Honest Cost Center

### 0:00 · The sentence nobody says
Here's a sentence nobody says out loud at the leadership offsite. Operations will never make you money. It's a cost center. It always was.
And yet it will happily eat unlimited amounts of your best people's time. The leave requests. The laptop nobody can find. The vendor contract nobody remembers signing. None of it grows the company. All of it has to happen.
Most companies respond by making ops bigger. That's the trap. The goal was never to make operations impressive. The goal is to make it small.

### 0:40 · Lean: name the waste
Toyota gave this problem its name decades ago. The Toyota Production System calls it waste: any activity that consumes resources but creates no value for the customer. Waiting. Handoffs. Rework. Chasing information that should already be in front of you. The customer never pays for any of it. You do.
Lean thinking is one move, repeated forever. Find the waste. Remove it. Keep the value. You don't need a factory to use it. You need an honest look at where your people's hours actually go. And in a modern company, most of the waste isn't on a production line. It's in the admin: the chasing, the reminding, the re-typing of things the company already knows.

### 1:20 · The ops wing of 8 Edges
Here's what small looks like. This is the operations wing of 8 Edges.
Leave lives on one board, not in anyone's inbox. Equipment lives in a register, not in someone's memory. Vendors live in one list, with the paperwork attached. Nothing here is clever. That's the point. Each screen exists to make a whole category of chasing disappear.
So what did the AI just do? The tracking, the filing, the record keeping. All of it. And what do your people still decide? The exceptions and the judgment calls. Only those.
Operations is a cost center, so we run it lean. Every hour it doesn't consume is money back, and an hour your best people spend on revenue or on building something new.

### 2:30 · Close
This week we make ops small, screen by screen. The busywork, the books, the bottleneck, and where the saved hours go. An operations function you barely notice, quietly doing everything it should. That's streamlined operations.

## Day 22 · Books That Sync Themselves

### 0:00 · Three weeks old
Quick question. Are your numbers current?
For most companies the honest answer is: they're three weeks old. The books close at month end. Someone reconciles. Someone builds the report. By the time it lands, the month it describes is history.
So every decision gets made looking in the rear-view mirror. With a delay. And someone on your team loses a Friday every month stitching the picture together by hand. That's the quiet cost nobody puts on the P and L.

### 0:40 · Leading, not lagging
Kaplan and Norton, the balanced scorecard researchers, drew the line that matters here. Lagging indicators versus leading indicators. Revenue is a lagging indicator. It tells you what already happened. Leading indicators tell you what's about to happen: the pipeline filling, the requests piling up, the metric drifting.
You can only watch the leading ones if the data flows continuously. A quarterly report can't show you a trend forming. A live number can. Stale books force you to manage on lag. Fresh books let you manage on lead. The fix isn't a better report. It's removing the human copy-paste between your accounting system and your decisions.

### 1:20 · The sync in 8 Edges
Here's how we killed the delay. The books sync from the accounting system on a schedule. Nobody copies numbers. Nobody spends Friday reconciling.
Every invoice lands in the same database as the deals, the people, and the goals. More than 150 tables. One home. So an invoice isn't just a number in the books. It's attached to the client, the deal it came from, and the goal it feeds. And the metrics page is never three weeks old. It's this week.
What did the AI just do? Pulled, matched, and filed every number. What do your people still decide? What the numbers mean, and what to do about them.
Finance ops is a cost center like the rest. Run it lean, and the hours you save on reconciling become hours spent on revenue and on building.

### 2:30 · Close
The month-end scramble didn't get faster. It disappeared. Numbers you can trust on the day you actually need them. That's streamlined operations.

## Day 23 · Find the Bottleneck

### 0:00 · Everything is urgent
Everything in your company feels urgent. It isn't.
There's a queue of fires and everyone's holding a hose. But at any moment, one constraint sets the pace for the whole system. One team, one step, one approval everything else is quietly waiting on. Fix anything else, and you've improved nothing. You've just polished a machine that's still waiting on its slowest part. The skill isn't working harder on everything. It's knowing which one thing is the thing.

### 0:40 · Goldratt's chain
Eliyahu Goldratt called this the Theory of Constraints, and taught it through a business novel called The Goal. A chain is only as strong as its weakest link, and a company is a chain.
The method is simple to say. Find the constraint. Get the most out of it. Point everything else at supporting it. Then strengthen it, and go find the next one.
Goldratt's sharpest point was about time. An hour lost at the bottleneck is an hour lost for the whole company. An hour saved anywhere else is an illusion. Which means most of the busywork your team calls urgent doesn't matter at all. And one thing matters completely.

### 1:20 · Finding it in 8 Edges
You don't find the constraint by feel. You find it in the numbers.
